## Why it exists

Every defence in the previous lesson can be turned against you by its own arithmetic. A service at 100 percent of its capacity that receives retries is pushed beyond it, and then everything it serves gets slower or fails, which triggers more retries. Seeing the arithmetic is how you design against it.

<Callout kind="why">
Outages are rarely one big failure. They are a small fault plus a feedback loop. Find the loops and cut them.
</Callout>

## How it works

### Amplification

A dependency can handle 100 requests per second. Clients send 100 per second, and each failed request is retried up to 3 times with no delay. During an outage, every request fails and is retried, so the offered load is 1 original plus 3 retries each:

```text file=amp.txt
offered load = 100 rps x (1 + 3 retries) = 400 rps  against a capacity of 100 rps
```

Four times the load on the service that is failing. Layers multiply: a client with 3 retries calling a gateway with 3 retries calling a service with 3 retries can turn one user action into 27 calls (the B7 lesson on retries). Exponential backoff with jitter reduces the rate; a **retry budget** (allow retries only up to, say, 10 percent of normal traffic) bounds the total no matter how many requests fail.

### Overload and queues

Little's law says concurrency equals throughput times latency. If latency rises because the service is saturated, concurrency (requests in flight) rises with it, consuming memory and connections. A queue in front adds delay: once the wait exceeds the client's timeout, the server is doing work nobody will read, and the useful throughput (goodput) falls toward zero. The system is busy and useless.

Defences:

- **Backpressure**: tell producers to slow down (reject, return 429/503, shrink a window) rather than buffering forever.
- **Bounded queues**: choose a maximum size or maximum wait; beyond it, reject quickly.
- **Load shedding**: drop low-priority or newest requests early to protect the rest. Failing 20 percent fast beats failing 100 percent slowly.
- **Deadlines**: drop queued work whose caller has already timed out.

### Staying broken

After the trigger is gone (a database restarted), a backlog and a retry wave can keep the service pinned above capacity. Recovery may need cutting traffic at the edge, shedding load, scaling out, or restarting with a cold cache protected by a ramp. This is why testing recovery, not only failure, matters.

### Degrade gracefully

Decide in advance what to turn off when stressed: recommendations, analytics writes, non-essential features, expensive search. Keep the core path (login, checkout) alive. This is a design decision made before the incident, with feature flags to apply it.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">
Adding more retries or longer queues to "make it more reliable". Both increase load on the failing part and delay the signal that something is wrong.
</Callout>

- **Retries at several layers**, multiplying attempts.
- **Unbounded queues** that hide overload until memory runs out.
- **Health checks that restart overloaded servers**, which adds a cold-start burst.
- **Autoscaling that reacts slowly** to a spike that has already caused a cascade.
- **Never rehearsing** an overload, so the first test is the real incident.

## Trade-offs

<Callout kind="tradeoff">
Shedding load deliberately fails some users to protect others, which is uncomfortable but better than failing everyone. Tight retry budgets reduce amplification and lower the success rate of transient errors. Choose limits from measured capacity.
</Callout>

## In production

<Callout kind="prod">
Alert on retry rate, queue age and goodput (successful, on-time responses), keep a runbook for "cut traffic, shed load, then restore gradually", and run game days where you inject latency and failures into a staging copy and watch whether the defences hold.
</Callout>

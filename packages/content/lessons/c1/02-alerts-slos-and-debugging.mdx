## Why it exists

Without a target you argue about whether the service is "fast enough" or "reliable enough" forever. Service-level objectives turn that into arithmetic, and a debugging method turns a 3 a.m. page into a sequence of steps instead of a panic.

<Callout kind="why">
Reliability is a feature with a budget. An SLO says how much unreliability is acceptable, which is also how much risk you may take with new releases.
</Callout>

## How it works

### SLI, SLO, error budget

- **SLI** (indicator): a measured ratio of good events to all events, such as "requests that return non-5xx within 300 ms".
- **SLO** (objective): the target for the SLI over a window, such as 99.5 percent over 28 days.
- **Error budget**: 100 percent minus the SLO. At 99.5 percent over 28 days, the budget is 0.5 percent of requests, or about 3.4 hours of total unavailability (28 days is 672 hours; 0.5 percent of that is 3.36 hours).

When the budget is healthy, ship faster. When it is spent, slow down and invest in reliability. This is how the product and operations sides negotiate with numbers.

### Alerting that people respect

Alert on **symptoms** users feel: error rate, latency percentiles, saturation approaching a limit, and **burn rate** (how fast the budget is being consumed: a fast burn pages, a slow burn opens a ticket). Do not page on causes ("CPU is 80 percent") unless they predict an imminent symptom. Each page needs a severity, an owner and a **runbook** link: what this alert means, how to check, and the first safe actions. If an alert fires and requires no action, delete or downgrade it.

### A debugging loop

1. **Scope**: who and what is affected? One route, one region, one customer, everyone? Use metrics sliced by those dimensions.
2. **Compare**: what changed? A deploy, a config change, a traffic spike, a dependency incident, an expired certificate. Check the deploy timeline first; most incidents follow a change.
3. **Hypothesise** from the signals: errors up with latency up suggests a slow dependency; errors with flat latency suggests a bad deploy.
4. **Test cheaply**: look at traces and logs for affected request ids; compare a failing and a succeeding request.
5. **Mitigate before you perfect**: roll back, turn off a feature flag, shed load. Restore service first, find the root cause after.
6. **Verify** the metric recovered, then write it up.

### Postmortems

After a significant incident write a **blameless** postmortem: timeline, impact, root causes (plural; there is rarely one), what went well, what was lucky, and action items with owners and dates. Blameless means asking "what let this happen" rather than "who did it". Track the actions to completion, otherwise the document changes nothing.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">
Treating the postmortem action list as a wish list. Unowned, undated actions never happen and the same incident returns.
</Callout>

- **Objectives set without looking at actual user needs or current performance.**
- **Paging on causes** and getting woken for harmless events.
- **No runbook**, so every page is a fresh investigation.
- **Fixing before mitigating**, extending the outage.
- **Skipping the postmortem for "small" incidents** where the lessons are cheapest.

## Trade-offs

<Callout kind="tradeoff">
Tighter SLOs cost more engineering and slow feature work; looser ones accept more user pain. Sensitive alerts catch problems early and risk fatigue. The right setting is the one users and on-call engineers can live with, revisited each quarter.
</Callout>

## In production

<Callout kind="prod">
Start with two or three SLOs (availability and latency of the main user journeys), burn-rate alerts with a fast and a slow window, a rotation with a handover note, a shared incident channel template, and a calendar slot to review action items.
</Callout>

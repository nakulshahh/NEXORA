## Why it exists

Without requirements and numbers, design discussions become opinions about technology. A method keeps the conversation on constraints.

<Callout kind="why">
Most designs fail by solving the wrong problem or the wrong scale. Ten minutes of arithmetic prevents both.
</Callout>

## How it works

1. **Requirements.** What must it do (functional)? How fast, available, durable, consistent (non-functional)? What is explicitly out of scope?
2. **Estimate.** Daily users × actions per user ÷ 86 400 s = average requests per second; multiply by a peak factor (2–5×). Storage = records × size × retention. Bandwidth = QPS × payload.
3. **Sketch the simplest design** (client, one service, one database) and **find the first bottleneck** against your numbers.
4. **Fix that bottleneck cheaply** (index, cache, read replica, queue) and repeat.
5. **Write down trade-offs and failure modes.**

Worked: 10 million daily users, 5 actions each = 5×10⁷ per day ≈ 580 requests/s average, perhaps 2 000/s at peak. One tuned database handles that for reads from an index; the first bottleneck is more likely a hot query than raw volume.

Keep the latency ladder in mind: memory reference ~100 ns, SSD read ~100 µs, same-datacentre round trip ~0.5 ms, cross-continent ~100+ ms (approximate, order of magnitude). A design that needs ten sequential cross-region calls cannot meet a 200 ms budget.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">
Starting with microservices, Kafka and Kubernetes before knowing the request rate.
</Callout>

- **Skipping non-functional requirements.**
- **Ignoring peaks and skew** (hot keys, celebrity users).
- **Designing only the happy path.**
- **No stated assumptions**, so nobody can challenge the numbers.

## Trade-offs

<Callout kind="tradeoff">
Simple designs are cheap to run and may need rework at scale; scalable designs are costly early. Choose by the growth you can credibly predict.
</Callout>

## In production

<Callout kind="prod">
Revisit estimates against real metrics after launch and keep short decision records so later engineers know why.
</Callout>

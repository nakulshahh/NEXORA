## Why it exists

A single process either works or crashes. A system of several machines can be half working: one node slow, a link dropped, a reply lost. Every later design chapter is a response to that.

<Callout kind="why">
Distribution is chosen for capacity or availability, and it costs you certainty. Know what you pay.
</Callout>

## How it works

### Partial failure and the ambiguous timeout

A client sends a payment request and the connection times out. Three worlds look identical: the request never arrived, it arrived and failed, or it succeeded and the reply was lost. You cannot tell from the client. So: make operations **idempotent** (B7), carry a key, and let the client retry safely.

### Quorums

With N replicas, a write waits for W acknowledgements and a read asks R replicas. If **R + W > N**, every read set overlaps every write set in at least one replica, so a read sees the latest acknowledged write (assuming a way to pick the newest). Checked by enumeration:

```text file=quorum.txt
N=3 W=2 R=2  overlap guaranteed
N=5 W=3 R=3  overlap guaranteed
N=3 W=1 R=1  no overlap (stale reads possible)
N=5 W=2 R=2  no overlap
```

Lower W or R gives lower latency and more availability, at the price of staleness.

### Logical time

Vector clocks keep one counter per node. Compare two: if every entry of A is ≤ B's and one is smaller, A happened **before** B; if each is ahead somewhere, they are **concurrent** (a conflict to resolve). Computed: `{A:2,B:1}` vs `{A:1,B:3}` are concurrent; `{A:1}` vs `{A:2,B:1}` is before.

### Consistency and the trade-off

During a network partition a system must choose: refuse some requests (consistency) or answer from possibly stale data (availability). That is CAP. PACELC adds that even without partitions you trade latency against consistency. **Consensus** protocols (Raft, Paxos) make a group agree on one order of operations, at the cost of coordination latency and needing a majority alive.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">
Using wall-clock timestamps to resolve conflicting writes ("last write wins"). With skewed clocks the older write can win and data is silently lost.
</Callout>

- **Assuming the network is reliable** or that latency is bounded.
- **Retrying non-idempotent operations.**
- **Treating "eventually consistent" as "correct soon" without defining conflicts.**
- **Distributing before a single node is exhausted.**

## Trade-offs

<Callout kind="tradeoff">
Strong consistency is simple to reason about and costs latency and availability. Weak consistency scales and pushes conflict handling onto the application.
</Callout>

## In production

<Callout kind="prod">
Test failure, not success: kill nodes, delay and drop packets, and verify invariants afterwards. Measure replication lag and alert on it.
</Callout>
